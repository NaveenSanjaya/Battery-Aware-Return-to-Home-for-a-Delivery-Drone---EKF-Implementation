\section{System overview}\label{sec:m_overview}
%--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Figure~\ref{fig:m_system} shows how the parts connect. The \textbf{simulator} flies the drone along its route and computes the ``true'' battery state with a detailed battery model. The \textbf{sensors} turn the true current and voltage into imperfect readings. The \textbf{EKF} sees only these readings and estimates the SoC and its uncertainty. The \textbf{decision rule} turns the estimate and its uncertainty into ``continue'' or ``return home''. Two \textbf{baselines}, charge counting and a voltage threshold, see the same sensor readings and make their own decisions. Finally, \textbf{scoring} uses the truth, which only the simulator knows, to judge every policy.

\section{Assumed drone and battery}\label{sec:m_assumptions}
%--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The drone carries a six-cell lithium-ion pack in series (6S) with a rated capacity of 5\,Ah. In a series pack the same current flows through every cell, so the project models one cell and takes the pack voltage as six times the cell voltage. All battery parameters and voltages in this report are per cell. Currents are pack currents, which are equal to the cell current. The filter and the simulator both run once per second ($\Delta t = 1$\,s).

Throughout the report the time index follows one convention: $I_k$ is the current drawn \emph{during} step $k$, and every state with index $k$ is its value at the \emph{end} of step $k$.

%--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Battery models: truth versus filter}\label{sec:m_truth_vs_filter}
%--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

A filter that is tested against its own model always looks good, and a real filter never has the exact model of a real battery. The simulator's ``true'' battery is therefore deliberately different from the model inside the EKF. This section describes both.

\subsection{Discrete-time RC dynamics}\label{sec:m_rc_discrete}

Both models contain RC pairs that obey \eqref{eq:bg_rc}. If the current is held constant at $I_k$ during a step of length $\Delta t$, \eqref{eq:bg_rc} can be solved exactly over the step:
\begin{equation}
  V_{i,k} = a_i\, V_{i,k-1} + R_i\,(1 - a_i)\, I_k,
  \qquad a_i = e^{-\Delta t / (R_i C_i)} .
  \label{eq:m_rc_discrete}
\end{equation}
The factor $a_i$ is the fraction of the old RC voltage that survives one step, and $R_i(1-a_i)I_k$ is how far the voltage moves towards its steady value $R_i I_k$. For the filter's pair ($\tau_1 = 30$\,s), $a_1 = e^{-1/30} \approx 0.967$.

\subsection{The true battery: two RC pairs}\label{sec:m_truth}

The simulated battery has a second, slow RC pair ($R_2$, $C_2$) that the filter does not know about. Its states are the SoC, $V_1$ and $V_2$, and they evolve as
\begin{align}
  \mathrm{SoC}_k &= \mathrm{SoC}_{k-1} - \frac{I_k\, \Delta t}{3600\, C_n}, \label{eq:m_true_soc}\\
  V_{1,k} &= a_1\, V_{1,k-1} + R_1 (1 - a_1)\, I_k, \label{eq:m_true_v1}\\
  V_{2,k} &= a_2\, V_{2,k-1} + R_2 (1 - a_2)\, I_k, \label{eq:m_true_v2}\\
  V_k &= \mathrm{OCV}(\mathrm{SoC}_k) + \delta_{\text{OCV}} - R_0\, I_k - V_{1,k} - V_{2,k}, \label{eq:m_true_v}
\end{align}
with all parameters at their true values (Table~\ref{tab:m_params}). Equation~\eqref{eq:m_true_v} is the terminal voltage at the end of the step; $\delta_{\text{OCV}}$ shifts the whole OCV curve. The battery starts at rest, so $V_{1,0} = V_{2,0} = 0$. The flight ends in failure if the terminal voltage falls below the cut-off voltage of 3.0\,V while current is flowing.

\subsection{The OCV curve}\label{sec:m_ocv}

Both models use the same OCV table (Table~\ref{tab:m_ocv}), a representative curve for a lithium-ion (NMC) cell: steep near empty and flatter in the middle. Values between the table points are found by shape-preserving piecewise cubic Hermite interpolation (MATLAB's \texttt{pchip}). It gives a smooth curve with a continuous slope and no overshoot, so the slope used by the EKF does not jump between table points.

The slope $d\,\mathrm{OCV}/d\,\mathrm{SoC}$, which the EKF needs, is computed by a central finite difference with a half-width of 0.005 (0.5\% SoC):
\begin{equation}
  \frac{d\,\mathrm{OCV}}{d\,\mathrm{SoC}}(s) \approx
  \frac{\mathrm{OCV}(s^{+}) - \mathrm{OCV}(s^{-})}{s^{+} - s^{-}},
  \qquad s^{+} = \min(s + 0.005,\, 1),\quad s^{-} = \max(s - 0.005,\, 0).
  \label{eq:m_ocv_slope}
\end{equation}
The $\min$ and $\max$ make the difference one-sided at the ends of the table, so the slope never uses points outside it.

\subsection{Why the two models differ}\label{sec:m_why_mismatch}

The mismatch makes the test believable and gives the filter a realistic challenge. The most visible difference is the slow pair. With $\tau_2 = 400$\,s, $V_2$ is still building up for most of a 15-minute flight, so the gap between the OCV and the terminal voltage slowly widens under steady load. The filter has no state for this slow drop and can only explain it with the states it has.

The size of the slow pair was revised during the project. The first setting was $R_2 = 4$\,m$\Omega$ ($C_2 = 100\,000$\,F), and the simulator test of Section~\ref{sec:r_sim} was run with it. Once all RC voltages have settled at a steady current, that setting gives a true total resistance of $R_0 + R_1 + R_2 = 18.2$\,m$\Omega$ against the filter's $R_0 + R_1 = 14$\,m$\Omega$, so at 15\,A the true battery would sit about 63\,mV lower than the filter's model expects. In the flat part of the OCV curve (5\,mV per 1\% SoC) this looks like an SoC error of up to about 13 percentage points, which is a harsher mismatch than a reasonably identified battery model would have. The default was therefore reduced to $R_2 = 1.5$\,m$\Omega$ with $C_2 = 266\,667$\,F, which keeps $\tau_2 = 400$\,s but makes the settled extra drop at 15\,A only $1.5\text{\,m}\Omega \times 15\text{\,A} \approx 23$\,mV. The original $R_2 = 4$\,m$\Omega$ is kept as the stress test in experiment E6 (Section~\ref{sec:m_experiments}), which measures how much of the gap ends up as SoC error and whether the uncertainty bands remain honest.

\section{Sensor model}\label{sec:m_sensors}
%--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The drone measures the current and the terminal voltage once per second:
\begin{align}
  I_{m,k} &= I_k + b + w_{I,k}, & w_{I,k} &\sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_I^2), \label{eq:m_sensor_i}\\
  V_{m,k} &= V_k + v_k,         & v_k &\sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_V^2). \label{eq:m_sensor_v}
\end{align}
The current sensor has a constant bias $b$ and random noise with standard deviation $\sigma_I = 0.2$\,A. In the Day~2 configuration $b = +0.5$\,A, so the sensor reads too high; the Monte Carlo study will draw $b$ between $-1$ and $+1$\,A. The voltage sensor has no bias and noise of $\sigma_V = 10$\,mV per cell. New noise samples are drawn independently at every step.

\section{Mission and drone model}\label{sec:m_mission}
%--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The drone starts at home, at position $(0, 0)$, and visits a list of drop-off points before returning. It moves through the flight phases in Table~\ref{tab:m_phases}. During cruise and return it flies in a straight line towards its next target at $v_{\text{cruise}} = 10$\,m/s. An RTH policy makes the drone turn back early simply by switching it to the return phase.

\begin{table}[htbp]
  \centering
  \caption{Flight phases and current demand (\texttt{sim/make\_mission.m}).}
  \label{tab:m_phases}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
    \toprule
    Phase & Duration & Current demand \\
    \midrule
    Take-off & 30\,s & 30\,A \\
    Cruise to next drop-off & Until arrival (10\,m/s) & $15\,\text{A} \times (1 + w)$ \\
    Hover at drop-off & 30\,s & 20\,A \\
    Return home & Until arrival (10\,m/s) & $15\,\text{A} \times (1 + w)$ \\
    Landing & 40\,s & 18\,A \\
    Landed & -- & 0\,A \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

Here $w$ is a wind factor: $w = 0.2$ means a headwind that needs 20\% more cruise current. It is zero in the Day~2 test; the Monte Carlo study will draw it within $\pm 20\%$. Two routes are defined. The \emph{short} route has three drop-offs at (1200, 400), (2200, $-400$) and (3000, 600)\,m and is meant to finish with charge to spare. The \emph{long} route has six drop-offs reaching 9\,km east of home and is designed to be too long for one battery, so that the RTH rule must act.

Real flight current is never perfectly constant: gusts and manoeuvres make it wander. The simulator adds a slowly varying random load $\varepsilon_k$ to the demand whenever the drone is flying:
\begin{equation}
  \varepsilon_k = 0.9\,\varepsilon_{k-1} + 0.3\, n_k, \qquad n_k \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1),
  \qquad I_k = \max\!\left(I_{\text{demand},k} + \varepsilon_k,\; 0\right).
  \label{eq:m_wobble}
\end{equation}
This first-order autoregressive process has a steady-state standard deviation of $0.3/\sqrt{1 - 0.9^2} \approx 0.7$\,A, and successive values are strongly correlated, so it looks like a slow wobble rather than white noise. The distance to home, used by the RTH rule, is the straight-line distance $d_{\text{home},k} = \lVert \mathbf{p}_k - \mathbf{p}_{\text{home}} \rVert$, where $\mathbf{p}_k$ is the drone's position; the GPS position is assumed exact.

\section{The Extended Kalman Filter}\label{sec:m_ekf}
%--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{States, input and measurement}\label{sec:m_ekf_states}

The filter's state vector holds five hidden quantities:
\begin{equation}
  x_k = \begin{bmatrix} \mathrm{SoC}_k & V_{1,k} & b_k & C_{n,k} & R_{0,k} \end{bmatrix}^{\top}.
  \label{eq:m_state}
\end{equation}
\begin{itemize}
  \item $\mathrm{SoC}$ is the quantity the drone actually needs.
  \item $V_1$ is needed to explain the measured voltage correctly when the load changes.
  \item $b$, the current-sensor bias, can be learned so that the filter removes the sensor's constant error.
  \item $C_n$, the capacity, can be learned so that the filter detects an aged battery. It also makes the prediction step nonlinear.
  \item $R_0$, the ohmic resistance, is learned because the design-stage investigation of Section~\ref{sec:m_ekf_investigation} showed that a wrong $R_0$ is the largest single source of SoC error. In a real battery $R_0$ also changes with temperature and age.
\end{itemize}
$R_0$ is placed last so that the first four states keep the positions they had in the original four-state design. Not every state is learned in every version of the filter: Section~\ref{sec:m_ekf_stages} defines three \emph{stages}, and a state that a stage does not learn is frozen at its nominal value. The input is the measured current $u_k = I_{m,k}$ and the measurement is the measured cell voltage $z_k = V_{m,k}$. The filter uses the nominal parameters of Table~\ref{tab:m_params} and one RC pair.

\subsection{Process model}\label{sec:m_ekf_process}

The filter's best guess of the true current is the \emph{bias-corrected current} $I_{m,k} - b_{k-1}$. Substituting it into the battery equations, and modelling $b$, $C_n$ and $R_0$ as constants that may wander slowly, gives the process model $x_k = f(x_{k-1}, u_k) + w_k$ in the form of \eqref{eq:bg_process}:
\begin{align}
  \mathrm{SoC}_k &= \mathrm{SoC}_{k-1} - \frac{(I_{m,k} - b_{k-1})\, \Delta t}{3600\, C_{n,k-1}}, \label{eq:m_f_soc}\\
  V_{1,k} &= a\, V_{1,k-1} + R_1 (1 - a)(I_{m,k} - b_{k-1}), \qquad a = e^{-\Delta t / (R_1 C_1)}, \label{eq:m_f_v1}\\
  b_k &= b_{k-1} + w_{b,k}, \label{eq:m_f_b}\\
  C_{n,k} &= C_{n,k-1} + w_{C,k}, \label{eq:m_f_cn}\\
  R_{0,k} &= R_{0,k-1} + w_{R,k}. \label{eq:m_f_r0}
\end{align}
In words: \eqref{eq:m_f_soc} is charge counting with the corrected current and the estimated capacity; \eqref{eq:m_f_v1} is the discrete RC equation \eqref{eq:m_rc_discrete}; and \eqref{eq:m_f_b}--\eqref{eq:m_f_r0} say ``these stay the same, apart from a slow random wander''. Modelling an unknown constant as a slow random walk is how a filter learns a constant it does not know. This is called \emph{state augmentation}.

\subsection{Measurement model}\label{sec:m_ekf_measurement}

The measured voltage follows the one-RC circuit equation \eqref{eq:bg_terminal}, with the bias-corrected current and the estimated resistance. In the form of \eqref{eq:bg_measurement}:
\begin{equation}
  V_{m,k} = h(x_k, u_k) + v_k = \mathrm{OCV}(\mathrm{SoC}_k) - V_{1,k} - R_{0,k}\,(I_{m,k} - b_k) + v_k .
  \label{eq:m_h}
\end{equation}
The model has three nonlinear parts: $\mathrm{OCV}(\mathrm{SoC})$ in the measurement, the product of the two states $R_0$ and $b$ in the measurement, and the division by the state $C_n$ in the prediction \eqref{eq:m_f_soc}.

\subsection{Prediction and the Jacobian F}\label{sec:m_ekf_f}

The predicted state is $\hat{x}_k^- = f(\hat{x}_{k-1}, u_k)$, using \eqref{eq:m_f_soc}--\eqref{eq:m_f_r0} without the noise terms. The Jacobian $F_k$ of \eqref{eq:bg_jacobians} is found by differentiating each line of $f$ with respect to each state, in the order of \eqref{eq:m_state}, and evaluating at $\hat{x}_{k-1}$:
\begin{equation}
  F_k = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} =
  \begin{bmatrix}
    1 & 0 & \dfrac{\Delta t}{3600\, \hat{C}_n} & \dfrac{(I_{m,k} - \hat{b})\, \Delta t}{3600\, \hat{C}_n^{2}} & 0 \\[8pt]
    0 & a & -R_1 (1 - a) & 0 & 0 \\[2pt]
    0 & 0 & 1 & 0 & 0 \\[2pt]
    0 & 0 & 0 & 1 & 0 \\[2pt]
    0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 1
  \end{bmatrix},
  \label{eq:m_F}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{b}$ and $\hat{C}_n$ are the estimates at step $k-1$. $F_k$ is the $5 \times 5$ identity matrix except for four entries, and each has a plain meaning.
\begin{itemize}
  \item Row 1, column 3: if the bias estimate is too high, the corrected current is too low, so the predicted SoC falls too slowly. A positive entry links a bias error to a positive SoC error.
  \item Row 1, column 4: a larger capacity makes the SoC fall more slowly. This entry depends on the current, which is why changes in current help the filter to separate bias from capacity.
  \item Row 2: $V_1$ decays by the factor $a$, and a bias error changes the current that drives it.
\end{itemize}
$R_0$ does not appear in the process model, so its column is the same as the identity's. The predicted covariance is then $P_k^- = F_k P_{k-1} F_k^{\top} + Q_k$, as in \eqref{eq:bg_kf_ppred}.

\subsection{Process noise Q from the current-sensor noise}\label{sec:m_ekf_q}

Most of the process noise has a known physical source: the random noise $w_{I,k}$ of the current sensor. By \eqref{eq:m_sensor_i}, the true current is $I_k = I_{m,k} - b - w_{I,k}$. The filter uses $I_{m,k} - b$ instead, so the true state evolves as $f(x_{k-1},\, I_{m,k} - w_{I,k})$. Because $f$ is linear in the measured current, this equals exactly
\begin{equation}
  x_k = f(x_{k-1}, I_{m,k}) - G_k\, w_{I,k},
  \qquad
  G_k = \frac{\partial f}{\partial I_m} =
  \begin{bmatrix} -\dfrac{\Delta t}{3600\, \hat{C}_n} & R_1 (1 - a) & 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}^{\top}.
  \label{eq:m_G}
\end{equation}
The current noise therefore enters the state through $G_k$ with covariance $G_k \sigma_I^2 G_k^{\top}$ (the minus sign disappears because it appears twice). Model errors that have no single physical source, and the slow wander of $b$, $C_n$ and $R_0$, are covered by small diagonal terms. The full process-noise covariance is
\begin{equation}
  Q_k = G_k\, \sigma_I^2\, G_k^{\top} + \mathrm{diag}\!\left(q_{\text{SoC}},\, q_{V_1},\, q_b,\, q_{C_n},\, q_{R_0}\right).
  \label{eq:m_Q}
\end{equation}
Three points are worth noting.
\begin{itemize}
  \item $G_k$ depends on the capacity estimate, so $Q_k$ is recomputed at every step.
  \item The first term is not diagonal. Its SoC--$V_1$ entry is negative: one burst of current noise lowers the true SoC and raises the true $V_1$ at the same time, and the filter knows that these two errors are linked.
  \item With the tuning values of Table~\ref{tab:m_tuning}, the derived term is small compared with the diagonal terms. For the SoC it is $(1/18\,000)^2 \times 0.2^2 \approx 1.2 \times 10^{-10}$ per step, against $q_{\text{SoC}} = 10^{-8}$. The diagonal terms will dominate unless tuning makes them smaller; experiment E5 explores this.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Update and the Jacobian H}\label{sec:m_ekf_h}

The voltage the filter expects is $\hat{z}_k = h(\hat{x}_k^-, u_k) = \mathrm{OCV}(\widehat{\mathrm{SoC}}_k^-) - \hat{V}_{1,k}^- - \hat{R}_{0,k}^- (I_{m,k} - \hat{b}_k^-)$. Differentiating \eqref{eq:m_h} with respect to the state gives
\begin{equation}
  H_k = \frac{\partial h}{\partial x} =
  \begin{bmatrix} \left.\dfrac{d\,\mathrm{OCV}}{d\,\mathrm{SoC}}\right|_{\widehat{\mathrm{SoC}}_k^-} & -1 & \hat{R}_{0,k}^- & 0 & -\left(I_{m,k} - \hat{b}_k^-\right) \end{bmatrix}.
  \label{eq:m_H}
\end{equation}
\begin{itemize}
  \item The first entry is the OCV slope at the predicted SoC, from \eqref{eq:m_ocv_slope}. It is large near empty and small in the flat middle, so the voltage corrects the SoC strongly near empty and weakly in the middle.
  \item The entry $+\hat{R}_0$ for the bias: a higher bias means a lower true current, so less sag and a higher voltage. Because $R_0$ is now a state, the current estimate $\hat{R}_0$ is used, not the nominal value.
  \item The entry 0 for the capacity: $C_n$ never appears in the voltage directly. The filter can learn it only through how the SoC evolves over time, through the cross-terms that $F_k$ builds up in $P$. This is why it learns slowly.
  \item The entry $-(I_m - \hat{b})$ for the resistance: more resistance means more sag, and the effect grows with the current. At 30\,A a 1\,m$\Omega$ error in $R_0$ changes the voltage by 30\,mV; at rest it changes nothing.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Measurement noise.} The measurement-noise variance has three parts:
\begin{equation}
  R = \sigma_V^2 + \left(R_0\, \sigma_I\right)^2 + \sigma_{\text{model}}^2 .
  \label{eq:m_Rv}
\end{equation}
The first term is the voltage-sensor noise. The second is the current-sensor noise seen through the resistance: the measured current appears in \eqref{eq:m_h}, so its noise of 0.2\,A adds $8\text{\,m}\Omega \times 0.2\text{\,A} = 1.6$\,mV to the voltage error. The third, $\sigma_{\text{model}}$, is an allowance for model error: the filter's one-RC model is never exact, so it should not trust its voltage prediction to better than about $\sigma_{\text{model}} = 10$\,mV. How this value was chosen is explained in Section~\ref{sec:m_ekf_investigation}. With the nominal values, $R \approx 2.0 \times 10^{-4}$\,V$^2$, equivalent to about 14\,mV of noise.

The update then follows \eqref{eq:bg_kf_innov}--\eqref{eq:bg_kf_xupd}. Because there is a single voltage measurement, $S_k = H_k P_k^- H_k^{\top} + R$ is a scalar, and the gain $K_k = P_k^- H_k^{\top} / S_k$ ($5 \times 1$) needs no matrix inverse. The covariance is updated in the Joseph form (Section~\ref{sec:m_ekf_safeguards}).

\subsection{Filter stages}\label{sec:m_ekf_stages}

Three versions of the filter are compared (Table~\ref{tab:m_stages}). All three use the same five-state code. A stage ``learns'' a state by giving it a non-zero initial variance and a non-zero process noise. A state that a stage does not learn is frozen at its nominal value ($b = 0$, $C_n = 5$\,Ah, $R_0 = 8$\,m$\Omega$) with zero initial variance and zero process noise. Its row and column of $P$ then stay zero for the whole flight, so its entry of the gain $K$ is zero and the update never moves it. This is mathematically the same as leaving the state out of the filter, but keeps one piece of code for all stages.

\subsection{Design-stage investigation: why R\texorpdfstring{$_0$}{0} became a state}\label{sec:m_ekf_investigation}

The original plan was a four-state filter, $[\mathrm{SoC}, V_1, b, C_n]$, built up in three stages: first $\mathrm{SoC}$ and $V_1$, then the bias, then the capacity. Before that design was fixed, it was tested in a Python port of the simulator that uses the same models and code structure. The numbers in this section come from that port. They are \textbf{design-stage checks} used to choose the filter structure, not the project's results, which come from the MATLAB code and are reported in Chapter~\ref{ch:results}. Unless stated otherwise, they are for experiment E1 (the short mission, true start at 95\%) with random seed~2.

\textbf{Step 1: the basic filter was biased and overconfident.} The two-state filter ($\mathrm{SoC}$, $V_1$) read the SoC too low, with an RMSE of 2.5\%, and the true SoC was inside its $\pm 3\sigma$ band only about 16\% of the time. Averaged over 20 seeds, without any model-error allowance ($\sigma_{\text{model}} = 0$), the RMSE was 2.45\% and the truth was inside the band 15\% of the time. A band that misses the truth most of the time is dangerous for the RTH rule, which relies on $\sigma_{\text{SoC}}$. For comparison, charge counting gave an RMSE of 1.96\% over the same 20 seeds: an EKF with the wrong model can be worse than the simple baseline.

\textbf{Step 2: finding the cause.} To find which difference between the true battery and the filter's model caused the error, the mismatches were switched on one at a time, with all the others switched off (Table~\ref{tab:m_ablation}). All cases used the default true battery, so the slow RC pair is the mild one ($R_2 = 1.5$\,m$\Omega$, $\tau_2 = 400$\,s).

The resistance mismatch is the largest error. The mechanism is simple: the true battery sags more than the filter expects, and the filter can only explain the extra drop by assuming a lower OCV, that is, a lower SoC. The effect is worst in the flat part of the OCV curve. At 15\,A, a 10\% error in $R_0$ is $0.8\text{\,m}\Omega \times 15\text{\,A} = 12$\,mV, which at 5\,mV per 1\% SoC looks like a 2.4\% lower SoC. The slow RC pair acts in the same way. The bias alone, in contrast, caused little error, because the voltage feedback keeps correcting the drift it causes; charge counting gave 1.17\% in the same case.

\textbf{Step 3: a larger measurement noise does not help.} One tempting fix is to make the filter trust the voltage less by increasing $R$. This gave an RMSE of 2.49\%, no better. A larger $R$ only makes the filter react more slowly. The resistance error is systematic, not random, so the voltage still points to the wrong SoC and the filter still drifts there. A Kalman filter assumes its errors are white noise, and a systematic error breaks that assumption. The model-error allowance of \eqref{eq:m_Rv} does not rescue the basic filter either: with $\sigma_{\text{model}} = 10$\,mV (E1, seed 2), stage~A gave an RMSE of 2.48\%, the truth was inside its $\pm 3\sigma$ band only 17\% of the time, and its mean NIS was 0.52.

\textbf{Step 4: learning $R_0$ fixes it.} Adding $R_0$ as an estimated state lets the filter explain the extra sag correctly. The sag changes with the current while the OCV does not, so the filter can separate the two: a voltage drop that appears only under heavy load must be resistance, not lower SoC. This became stage~B.

\textbf{Step 5: the bias and capacity could not be learned in one flight.} Adding $b$ and $C_n$ as well did not work within one 14-minute flight. The bias estimate ended at $-0.28$\,A when the true value was $+0.5$\,A, and with all five states the SoC RMSE rose to 1.96\%. The reason is \emph{observability}. From the $b$ and $R_0$ entries of $H$ in \eqref{eq:m_H}, small errors $\delta b$ and $\delta R_0$ change the predicted voltage by
\begin{equation}
  \delta V \approx \hat{R}_0\, \delta b - (I_m - \hat{b})\, \delta R_0 .
  \label{eq:m_confound}
\end{equation}
When the current is nearly constant, the combination $\delta b = \frac{I_m - \hat{b}}{\hat{R}_0}\, \delta R_0$ leaves the voltage unchanged, so the measurements cannot tell a bias error from a resistance error. Only changes in current separate them. The capacity is even harder: it does not appear in $h$ at all and shows only through slow SoC drift, which the bias also causes. One flight with mostly steady cruise does not contain enough information to separate all three. Stage~C was therefore first planned only as an observability study (experiments E3 and E4). Section~\ref{sec:m_ekf_choice} shows why it nevertheless became the filter used for decisions.

\textbf{Step 6: choosing the model-error allowance.} With $R_0$ learned, the remaining model errors (the slow RC pair, the OCV offset and the other parameter offsets) are still systematic. The allowance $\sigma_{\text{model}}$ in \eqref{eq:m_Rv} tells the filter about them. Table~\ref{tab:m_sigmodel} compares three values for stage~B. A mean NIS near 1 means the innovations have the size the filter expects; below 1 means the filter is more cautious than necessary.

The value $\sigma_{\text{model}} = 10$\,mV was chosen, and it is used for all three stages in the MATLAB experiments. It makes the filter slightly conservative (mean NIS about 0.5), but its $\pm 3\sigma$ band then contains the truth 98\% of the time, close to the 99.7\% expected of an honest Gaussian band. Honest bands matter more here than a NIS of exactly 1, because the RTH rule turns $\sigma_{\text{SoC}}$ directly into a safety margin. This does not contradict Step~3. There, a larger $R$ could not remove a large systematic error. Here, the large error has already been removed by learning $R_0$, and the allowance only makes the stated uncertainty cover the smaller errors that remain.

\subsection{Choosing the filter for decisions}\label{sec:m_ekf_choice}

The investigation above chose stage~B as the most accurate filter on a healthy battery. The RTH rule, however, will face batteries that are not healthy: the Monte Carlo study includes aged batteries and biased sensors (Table~\ref{tab:m_mc_factors}). Before the final experiments, all three stages and charge counting were therefore compared on the five scenarios of experiment E8, again in the Python port (Table~\ref{tab:m_stagecheck}). These are \textbf{design-stage checks}; the MATLAB results of E8 are reported in Section~\ref{sec:r_e8}.

\textbf{Stage B fails on an aged battery, in the dangerous direction.} With a battery at 85\% of its rated capacity, the true SoC falls faster than stage~B predicts, so the true OCV, and the voltage, end up lower than expected. During steady cruise, stage~B can explain this shortfall either by a lower SoC or by a higher resistance (Section~\ref{sec:m_ekf_investigation}, Step~5). It explains it with its $R_0$ state. Its SoC estimate therefore stays too high: in the design check it overestimated the SoC by about 8\% at landing (final error $+8.3\%$), and its band contained the truth only 27\% of the time. An overestimated SoC is the dangerous direction for a drone, because it turns back too late.

\textbf{Stage C stays honest.} In scenarios E1--E4 its band contained the truth 100\% of the time, with an RMSE between 1.0\% and 1.8\%. It is slightly less accurate than stage~B on a healthy battery, because it has more unknowns to share the same information.

\textbf{Its parameter estimates are individually wrong, but their combined effect is right.} In single runs (seed 2), stage~C's bias estimate in E3 ended at $-0.03$\,A when the true bias was $+0.8$\,A. In E4 its capacity estimate moved from 5.0 towards the true 4.25\,Ah, ending at 4.49\,Ah, while its bias estimate ended at $-0.80$\,A against a true $+0.5$\,A. Yet its SoC stayed accurate. The reason is in the process model \eqref{eq:m_f_soc}: the bias and the capacity affect the SoC only through the discharge rate
\begin{equation}
  r = \frac{I_m - b}{C_n},
  \label{eq:m_rate}
\end{equation}
and the voltage, through the SoC, constrains only this rate. Many pairs $(b, C_n)$ give the same rate at the currents of one flight, so the rate is observable while $b$ and $C_n$ separately are not. In the E4 run above, the wrong-signed bias makes the estimated corrected current too large, which compensates for the capacity estimate still being too high. At a true current of 15\,A, the filter's estimated rate is $(15 + 0.5 + 0.80)/4.49 \approx 3.63$ per hour against the true $15/4.25 \approx 3.53$ per hour: only about 3\% apart, although the bias is 1.3\,A wrong.

\textbf{Decision.} Stage~C will drive the RTH decision. The RTH rule turns $\sigma_{\text{SoC}}$ directly into a safety margin, and the Monte Carlo study includes aged batteries, so a filter whose uncertainty stays honest matters more than the last 0.5\% of accuracy on a healthy battery. The lesson is that \emph{the most accurate filter is not always the safest one to make decisions with}. Stage~B remains the most accurate estimator of a healthy battery, and both are reported in Chapter~\ref{ch:results}.

Two cheap additions would make $b$ and $C_n$ individually observable, and are left as future work: calibrating the current-sensor bias at rest before take-off, when the true current is known to be zero; and estimating the capacity between flights, from the charge drawn (ampere-hours) and the SoC read from the resting voltage before and after the flight.

In the strong-mismatch scenario E6 all filters degrade, and even the best band (stage~C) contains the truth only 81\% of the time. This is the honest limit of the method: when the model is too wrong, the EKF becomes biased and overconfident. Possible remedies are a two-RC filter model, a larger $\sigma_{\text{model}}$, or adaptive noise estimation.

\subsection{Initialisation and tuning}\label{sec:m_ekf_init}

The initial estimate is
\begin{equation}
  \hat{x}_0 = \begin{bmatrix} \widehat{\mathrm{SoC}}_0 & 0 & 0 & 5.0\text{\,Ah} & 8\text{\,m}\Omega \end{bmatrix}^{\top}:
  \label{eq:m_x0}
\end{equation}
no polarisation (the battery has rested), no known bias, and the nominal capacity and resistance. In normal use the starting SoC is read from the resting voltage before take-off. The battery is at rest, so the measured voltage is the OCV plus sensor noise, and an inverse lookup of the OCV table gives the SoC. Its uncertainty is then small, $\pm 3\%$ (one standard deviation). In the Day~4 experiments (E3--E8, including the E1 and E2 scenarios repeated in E8) the resting voltage is the average of 10 readings, which reduces its noise by $\sqrt{10} \approx 3$, to about 3\,mV, or roughly 0.3\% SoC near full charge, where the curve rises by about 10\,mV per 1\% SoC. The Day~3 runs of E1 and E2 (Sections~\ref{sec:r_e1} and~\ref{sec:r_e2}) used a single reading, and the start about 3\% low seen there motivated this change. The wrong-start test (E2) instead assumes a full battery with a large uncertainty of $\pm 20\%$, to see how the filter recovers.

The initial uncertainty (one standard deviation) of the other states is $\pm 10$\,mV for $V_1$, $\pm 1$\,A for the bias, $\pm 0.5$\,Ah for the capacity and $\pm 4$\,m$\Omega$ for the resistance, so for a stage that learns all states
\begin{equation}
  P_0 = \mathrm{diag}\!\left(\sigma_{\text{SoC},0}^2,\; 0.01^2,\; 1^2,\; 0.5^2,\; 0.004^2\right).
  \label{eq:m_P0}
\end{equation}
For stages A and B, the entries of the states they do not learn are set to zero. The tuning values are listed in Table~\ref{tab:m_tuning}; experiment E5 tests how sensitive the filter is to them.

\subsection{Numerical safeguards}\label{sec:m_ekf_safeguards}

\begin{itemize}
  \item The covariance is updated in the \emph{Joseph form}~\cite{simon2006optimal},
  \begin{equation}
    P_k = (\mathbb{I} - K_k H_k)\, P_k^-\, (\mathbb{I} - K_k H_k)^{\top} + K_k R K_k^{\top},
    \label{eq:m_joseph}
  \end{equation}
  instead of the short form \eqref{eq:bg_kf_pupd}. Both are equal in exact arithmetic, but the Joseph form keeps $P$ symmetric and positive definite despite rounding errors. $P$ is also symmetrised afterwards, $P \leftarrow (P + P^{\top})/2$.
  \item After each update, the SoC is clamped to $[0, 1]$, the capacity is kept at or above 0.5\,Ah and the resistance at or above 0.1\,m$\Omega$, so that no physically impossible value can enter the next step.
  \item A one-sided slope is used at the ends of the OCV table, as in \eqref{eq:m_ocv_slope}.
  \item SoC is kept as a fraction, time in seconds and capacity in Ah inside all equations; this is the origin of the factor 3600.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Checking that the filter is honest}\label{sec:m_ekf_consistency}

A filter can be accurate yet overconfident, which is dangerous for a decision rule that relies on its uncertainty. Four checks are used.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{$\pm 3\sigma$ bands.} If the uncertainty is correct and the errors are Gaussian, the true error should stay inside $\pm 3\sqrt{P_{ii}}$ about 99.7\% of the time.
  \item \textbf{Innovations.} They should look like zero-mean random noise with no trend, inside about $\pm 3\sqrt{S_k}$.
  \item \textbf{Normalised innovation squared (NIS).} $\epsilon_{\text{NIS},k} = \tilde{y}_k^2 / S_k$ should average about 1 (the number of measurements). A much larger average means the filter is overconfident; a much smaller one means it is too cautious.
  \item \textbf{Normalised estimation error squared (NEES).} $\epsilon_{\text{NEES},k} = e_k^{\top} P_k^{-1} e_k$, with $e_k = x_k - \hat{x}_k$, should average about the number of states compared. It needs the true state, so it can only be computed in simulation.
\end{itemize}
The NEES is computed on the SoC alone, $\epsilon_{\text{NEES},k} = e_{\text{SoC},k}^2 / P_{k,11}$, with an expected average of 1. This is the state the RTH rule uses, and it avoids two problems with the full state: the filter's $V_1$ has no true counterpart (the true battery has two RC pairs), and frozen states have zero variance, so $P$ cannot be inverted over them.

%--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Risk-aware return-to-home rule}\label{sec:m_rth}
%--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Every second, the drone computes the probability that it can still reach home with a safety reserve. When this probability drops below a threshold $p^{\ast} = 95\%$, it turns back. The SoC estimate and its uncertainty come from the stage-C filter (Section~\ref{sec:m_ekf_choice}). The rule is tested on the \emph{long} route (six drop-offs, Section~\ref{sec:m_mission}), which is too far for one battery, so every mission needs a turn-back decision.

\subsection{What getting home costs}\label{sec:m_rth_cost}

The drone flies home in a straight line at $v_{\text{cruise}} = 10$\,m/s and then lands, which takes $t_{\text{land}} = 40$\,s at $I_{\text{land}} = 18$\,A (the landing phase of the simulator). The cruise time home is
\begin{equation}
  t_{\text{cruise},k} = \frac{d_{\text{home},k}}{v_{\text{cruise}}} .
  \label{eq:m_thome}
\end{equation}
The charge this needs is computed with the same drain rate that the filter uses, $(I_m - \hat{b})/\hat{C}_n$. Day~4 showed that this combination is what the voltage makes observable, while $\hat{b}$ and $\hat{C}_n$ separately are not reliable (Section~\ref{sec:m_ekf_choice}, Equation~\eqref{eq:m_rate}). Using $\hat{C}_n$ on its own with a nominal current could misjudge the trip for an aged battery. The SoC needed to get home is therefore
\begin{equation}
  \Delta\mathrm{SoC}_{\text{home},k} = \frac{\left(\hat{I}_{\text{cr},k} - \hat{b}_k\right) t_{\text{cruise},k} + I_{\text{land}}\, t_{\text{land}}}{3600\, \hat{C}_{n,k}} ,
  \label{eq:m_dsoc_home}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{I}_{\text{cr},k}$ is the cruise current \emph{as the sensor measures it}, learned during the flight with an exponential moving average that is updated only in cruise:
\begin{equation}
  \hat{I}_{\text{cr},k} = 0.98\, \hat{I}_{\text{cr},k-1} + 0.02\, I_{m,k}, \qquad \hat{I}_{\text{cr},0} = 15\text{\,A}.
  \label{eq:m_icr}
\end{equation}
The weight of 0.02 per second gives a memory of about 50\,s. The learned current therefore includes the effect of the wind and the sensor bias, which the filter's $\hat{b}$ then removes, just as in the process model \eqref{eq:m_f_soc}. The two terms are treated differently on purpose: $\hat{I}_{\text{cr}}$ is a measured current, so the estimated bias is removed from it, while $I_{\text{land}}$ is the nominal true landing current, which contains no sensor bias and is used as it is.

A reserve of $\mathrm{SoC}_{\text{res}} = 10\%$ is always kept for landing safety. For example, with no bias and a 5\,Ah battery, at 3.2\,km from home the drone needs $(15\text{\,A} \times 320\text{\,s} + 18\text{\,A} \times 40\text{\,s})/3600 \approx 1.53$\,Ah, about 31\% SoC.

\subsection{From margin to probability}\label{sec:m_rth_prob}

The \emph{safety margin} is the charge left over after the trip home and the reserve:
\begin{equation}
  m_k = \widehat{\mathrm{SoC}}_k - \Delta\mathrm{SoC}_{\text{home},k} - \mathrm{SoC}_{\text{res}} .
  \label{eq:m_margin}
\end{equation}
The margin is uncertain for two reasons. The first is the SoC estimate itself, with standard deviation $\sigma_{\text{SoC},k} = \sqrt{P_{k,11}}$ taken straight from the EKF covariance. The second is surprises on the trip home, such as a change in wind or load. These grow with the length of the trip, so their standard deviation is taken as a fixed fraction of the trip cost, $\sigma_{\text{trip},k} = f_{\text{trip}}\, \Delta\mathrm{SoC}_{\text{home},k}$ with $f_{\text{trip}} = 0.10$. For comparison, a wind effect spread uniformly over $\pm 20\%$ (Table~\ref{tab:m_mc_factors}) has a standard deviation of $0.2/\sqrt{3} \approx 11.5\%$, so 10\% is of the same order; the learned cruise current \eqref{eq:m_icr} already absorbs part of the wind. Treating the two sources as independent and Gaussian, they combine into the total standard deviation \eqref{eq:m_sigma_total}, which converts the margin into a probability \eqref{eq:m_psafe}:
\begin{align}
  \sigma_{\text{total},k} &= \sqrt{\sigma_{\text{SoC},k}^2 + \left(f_{\text{trip}}\, \Delta\mathrm{SoC}_{\text{home},k}\right)^2}, \label{eq:m_sigma_total}\\
  P_{\text{safe},k} &= \Phi\!\left(\frac{m_k}{\sigma_{\text{total},k}}\right), \qquad
  \Phi(z) = \tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathrm{erfc}\!\left(-\frac{z}{\sqrt{2}}\right). \label{eq:m_psafe}
\end{align}
$\Phi$ is the standard normal cumulative distribution function, written with the complementary error function $\mathrm{erfc}$ so that it needs only base MATLAB. The drone returns home at the first step where $P_{\text{safe},k} < p^{\ast}$.

For $p^{\ast} = 0.95$, $\Phi(1.645) = 0.95$, so the rule is the same as ``return when $m_k < 1.645\,\sigma_{\text{total},k}$''. It keeps a safety buffer of 1.645 standard deviations. When the filter is confident the buffer is small and the drone flies longer; when it is unsure the buffer grows automatically and the drone turns back earlier. Table~\ref{tab:m_rth_example} illustrates this with two filters that give the same estimate but different uncertainties.

A rule that used only the estimate would treat both cases the same. The risk-aware rule does not, and that is the reason for estimating the uncertainty at all.

\textbf{What the threshold promises.} If $\sigma_{\text{total}}$ is honest, $P_{\text{safe}} \geq p^{\ast}$ at the moment of turning back means that the drone should land with the reserve intact in at least a fraction $p^{\ast}$ of missions. The share of missions that break the reserve should therefore be at most $1 - p^{\ast}$, for example 5\% for $p^{\ast} = 0.95$. This gives a direct test of the whole chain (filter, trip cost and probability) in the Monte Carlo study. A design-stage check in the Python port suggested that this promise holds: at every threshold tried, the share of missions that broke the reserve stayed at or below $1 - p^{\ast}$.

The rule rests on stated assumptions: a straight flight home, a cruise current that stays at its learned value, and Gaussian errors. The uncertainty of the drain rate itself (through $\hat{b}$ and $\hat{C}_n$) is not added to $\sigma_{\text{total}}$; only the SoC uncertainty and the trip allowance are.

\subsection{The four policies compared}\label{sec:m_policies}

Four turn-back policies are compared on the same missions (Table~\ref{tab:m_policies}). Policies A and B are baselines that a simple drone could use; C and D both use the stage-C EKF, and differ only in whether they use its uncertainty. All policies are checked only while the drone is working (cruising or hovering at a drop-off), not during take-off or once it is already returning. When a policy fires, the drone abandons the remaining deliveries, flies straight home and lands.

The voltage threshold of policy A was set to 3.5\,V after a design-stage check in the Python port (60 missions). With 3.4\,V the battery died in 92\% of missions, with 3.5\,V in 10\%, and with 3.6\,V in none, but then 27\% of the battery was left unused. No fixed voltage is both safe and efficient, because the voltage at which the drone must turn back depends on how far from home it is and on the battery's state. Policy A is also swept over 3.40--3.60\,V in the threshold sweep.

\subsection{How a mission is scored}\label{sec:m_scoring}

After a policy decides to turn back, the simulator flies the drone home on the true battery. Each mission is then scored on four measures.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Died:} the cell voltage under load fell below 3.0\,V, or the true SoC reached zero, before landing.
  \item \textbf{Reserve broken:} the drone died, or landed with less than the 10\% reserve. This is the event that policy D promises to avoid with probability $p^{\ast}$.
  \item \textbf{Unused battery:} the true SoC at landing minus the 10\% reserve, averaged over the missions that landed (it is negative for a mission that landed below the reserve). Lower means the battery was used better, provided the reserve was kept.
  \item \textbf{Deliveries:} the number of drop-offs completed before turning back.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Monte Carlo study.} The four policies are compared on 500 random missions on the long route. The random conditions of each mission are drawn once, uniformly within the ranges of Table~\ref{tab:m_mc_factors}, and every policy flies exactly the same conditions with exactly the same sensor noise (seed $10\,000 + i$ for mission $i$), so the differences come from the policies alone. Before take-off the starting SoC is read from 10 averaged resting readings, and both the stage-C EKF and charge counting start from that guess. For each policy the study reports the share of missions in which the battery died, the share in which the reserve was broken, the mean unused battery and the mean number of deliveries (Section~\ref{sec:m_scoring}), and the share of deaths among aged batteries (capacity below 90\%).

The working hypothesis is that policy D breaks the reserve in no more than about 5\% of missions while wasting little battery; that C, which aims exactly at the reserve, breaks it often; that B dies when the battery is aged or the sensor reads low; and that A either wastes battery or dies, depending on its threshold. If the results differ, they will be reported and explained.

\textbf{Threshold sweep.} On the first 200 missions, policy D is rerun with $p^{\ast} =$ 80\%, 90\%, 95\%, 99\% and 99.9\%, and policy A with $V_{\text{thr}} =$ 3.40, 3.45, 3.50, 3.55 and 3.60\,V. Plotting the share of missions that broke the reserve against the mean unused battery gives a trade-off curve for each policy (bottom-left is best). It shows an operator what each extra ``nine'' of safety costs in range, and whether any voltage threshold can match the risk-aware rule.

\textbf{Demonstration mission.} Before the Monte Carlo study, one mission is flown with all four policies under deliberately difficult conditions: start at 95\%, an aged battery (85\% capacity), a current sensor reading 0.5\,A low, and a 10\% headwind. It shows how each policy decides on a single flight.
