\section{Battery basics}\label{sec:bg_battery}
%--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

\label{sec:bg_soc}The \emph{capacity} $C_n$ is the charge a full battery can deliver, in ampere-hours (Ah); it falls as the battery ages. The \emph{state of charge} (SoC) is the fraction of the capacity that remains, from 1 (full) to 0 (empty).

\label{sec:bg_ocv}When a battery rests, its voltage depends only on its SoC. This resting voltage is the \emph{open-circuit voltage} (OCV). The OCV curve (Figure~\ref{fig:bg_ocv_tangent}) is curved, so a straight-line model cannot describe it, and its slope changes: about 25\,mV per 1\% SoC near empty, but only 5\,mV per 1\% between 20\% and 40\%. Where the curve is flat, the voltage says little about the SoC, so a good filter should become less certain there.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{fig2_ocv_curve.png}
  \caption{OCV curve of one cell. It is steep near empty and flat between about 20\% and 50\% SoC. The dashed tangent at 40\% SoC is what the EKF uses: close to the curve nearby, wrong far away.}
  \label{fig:bg_ocv_tangent}
\end{figure}

\label{sec:bg_r0}\label{sec:bg_rc}\label{sec:bg_ecm}Under load the voltage drops in two ways. An instant drop $R_0 I$ comes from the internal resistance $R_0$, where $I$ is the current (positive for discharge):
\begin{equation}
  \Delta V_{R_0} = R_0\, I .
  \label{eq:bg_sag}
\end{equation}
At 30\,A take-off current and $R_0 = 8$\,m$\Omega$ this is 0.24\,V per cell, so the battery looks emptier than it is. A slower drop $V_1$ builds up over tens of seconds, modelled by a resistor $R_1$ in parallel with a capacitor $C_1$:
\begin{equation}
  C_1 \frac{dV_1}{dt} = I - \frac{V_1}{R_1},
  \label{eq:bg_rc}
\end{equation}
with time constant $\tau_1 = R_1 C_1$ (30\,s here). Together these form the equivalent circuit of Figure~\ref{fig:bg_circuit}. The sensor reads
\begin{equation}
  V = \mathrm{OCV}(\mathrm{SoC}) - R_0\, I - V_1 .
  \label{eq:bg_terminal}
\end{equation}
Only $V$ and $I$ are measured; the SoC and $V_1$ must be estimated.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{fig3_equivalent_circuit.png}
  \caption{One-RC equivalent circuit used by the EKF. The simulated ``true'' battery adds a second, slower RC pair (Section~\ref{sec:m_truth_vs_filter}).}
  \label{fig:bg_circuit}
\end{figure}

\label{sec:bg_cc}The simplest SoC tracker is \emph{charge counting}: start from a known SoC and subtract the charge drawn. With time step $\Delta t$ (s) and current $I_k$ (A),
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{SoC}_{k} = \mathrm{SoC}_{k-1} - \frac{I_k\, \Delta t}{3600\, C_n}.
  \label{eq:bg_cc}
\end{equation}
It never corrects itself, so a wrong start stays wrong, a wrong capacity makes it drift, and a current-sensor bias $b$ adds an error that grows with time:
\begin{equation}
  e(t) = -\frac{b\, t}{3600\, C_n}.
  \label{eq:bg_cc_drift}
\end{equation}
A 0.5\,A bias over 15 minutes is 2.5\% of a 5\,Ah battery. Charge counting is used as a baseline.

\section{From the Kalman filter to the Extended Kalman Filter}\label{sec:bg_kf}
%--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

\label{sec:bg_kf_idea}\label{sec:bg_ss}A Kalman filter balances a prediction against a measurement. For the drone, charge counting \eqref{eq:bg_cc} is the prediction and the voltage \eqref{eq:bg_terminal} is the measurement. The hidden quantities form the state $x_k$; the measured current is the input $u_k$ and the measured voltage is the measurement $z_k$:
\begin{equation}
  x_k = f(x_{k-1}, u_k) + w_k, \qquad z_k = h(x_k, u_k) + v_k,
  \label{eq:bg_process}
\end{equation}
where $w_k$ and $v_k$ are zero-mean, bell-shaped (Gaussian) errors with covariances $Q$ (model error) and $R$ (sensor error). The filter keeps an estimate $\hat{x}_k$ and its uncertainty (covariance) $P_k$.

\label{sec:bg_kf_loop}For a linear model, $f = F x + B u$ and $h = H x$, each step has two parts. \textbf{Predict} with the model (uncertainty grows), then \textbf{update} with the measurement (uncertainty shrinks):
\begin{align}
  \hat{x}_k^- &= F \hat{x}_{k-1} + B u_k, \qquad P_k^- = F P_{k-1} F^{\top} + Q, \label{eq:bg_kf_ppred}\\
  \tilde{y}_k &= z_k - H \hat{x}_k^-, \qquad S_k = H P_k^- H^{\top} + R, \label{eq:bg_kf_innov}\\
  K_k &= P_k^- H^{\top} S_k^{-1}, \qquad \hat{x}_k = \hat{x}_k^- + K_k \tilde{y}_k, \label{eq:bg_kf_xupd}\\
  P_k &= (\mathbb{I} - K_k H) P_k^- . \label{eq:bg_kf_pupd}
\end{align}
Here ``$-$'' means before the measurement, $\tilde{y}_k$ is the \emph{innovation} (the filter's surprise), $S_k$ its expected variance, and $K_k$ the \emph{Kalman gain}. \label{eq:bg_kf_s}\label{sec:bg_gain}The gain is large when the prediction is uncertain and small when the sensor is noisy or the measurement says little about the state, as on the flat part of the OCV curve. \label{sec:bg_qr}$Q$ and $R$ are the tuning knobs: a large $Q$ makes the filter fast but jumpy, a large $R$ smooth but slow.

\label{sec:bg_ekf}The battery model is not linear, because of $\mathrm{OCV}(\mathrm{SoC})$. The EKF replaces $f$ and $h$, at every step, by their tangent at the current estimate. The slopes form the \emph{Jacobians}
\begin{equation}
  F_k = \left.\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\right|_{\hat{x}_{k-1},\,u_k},
  \qquad
  H_k = \left.\frac{\partial h}{\partial x}\right|_{\hat{x}_k^-,\,u_k},
  \label{eq:bg_jacobians}
\end{equation}
which replace $F$ and $H$ in \eqref{eq:bg_kf_ppred}--\eqref{eq:bg_kf_pupd}, while the state and expected measurement use the full nonlinear $f$ and $h$. For the battery the key entry of $H_k$ is the OCV slope at the current SoC estimate, the tangent in Figure~\ref{fig:bg_ocv_tangent}. This works well when the curve is smooth and the uncertainty small, as here~\cite{simon2006optimal,welch2006intro}.

\section{A worked example}\label{sec:bg_example}
%--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

One EKF step with SoC as the only state (\texttt{learning/day1\_ekf\_by\_hand.m}) shows the gain at work. The filter starts at $80\% \pm 5\%$. Drawing 20\,A for 10\,s predicts 78.89\%. The model then expects 3.832\,V, but the sensor reads 3.80\,V, a surprise of $-32$\,mV. With an OCV slope of 7\,mV per 1\% SoC and a 10\,mV sensor, the gain is $K = 1.32$\,V$^{-1}$, and the estimate moves to $74.6\% \pm 1.4\%$: one voltage reading cut the uncertainty from 5\% to 1.4\%. With a noisy 50\,mV sensor, or a confident $\pm 1\%$ start, the gain falls to about 0.47 and the estimate moves much less.

\section{Related work}\label{sec:bg_related}
%--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Plett's three-part paper~\cite{plett2004part1,plett2004part2,plett2004part3} is the classic reference for EKF battery estimation, including adding unknown parameters such as resistance or capacity to the state, as done here; his textbook~\cite{plett2015bms} covers equivalent-circuit models in depth. MathWorks provides a MATLAB EKF battery example~\cite{mathworks_ekf_battery}. Simon~\cite{simon2006optimal} covers the KF, EKF, unscented Kalman filter (UKF) and particle filter, and Welch and Bishop~\cite{welch2006intro} give a short introduction.

For drones, an EKF has been used for UAV battery SoC estimation~\cite{hal_uav_ekf}, and prediction of remaining flying time, the quantity an RTH decision depends on, has been verified for electric unmanned vehicles~\cite{ijphm_flight_time}. Dibbern et al.~\cite{purdue_jate_comparison} compared an EKF, a UKF and charge counting on delivery and inspection missions, and found the UKF more accurate and parameter identification more important than the load profile. Public flight and cell datasets~\cite{cmu_dji_dataset,cmu_evtol_dataset,cmu_evtol_paper,mcmaster_lg_hg2} are available for future validation; this project uses simulation so that the true SoC is always known.
