\section{How a battery behaves}\label{sec:bg_battery}
%--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

\label{sec:bg_soc}\textbf{Capacity and state of charge.} The \emph{capacity} $C_n$ is the total charge a full battery can deliver, in ampere-hours (Ah); a 5\,Ah battery can supply 5\,A for one hour. The capacity falls as a battery ages. The \emph{state of charge} (SoC) is the fraction of the capacity that is left, from 1 (full) to 0 (empty). Equations use fractions; the text gives the SoC and its errors in percentage points of SoC.

\label{sec:bg_ocv}\textbf{Resting voltage.} When no current flows, the battery voltage depends only on the SoC. This is the \emph{open-circuit voltage} (OCV), shown in Figure~\ref{fig:bg_ocv_tangent}. The curve is non-linear, which is why an \emph{Extended} Kalman Filter is used, and its slope varies: near empty the voltage changes by about 25\,mV per 1\% of SoC, but between 20\% and 50\% by only 5\,mV. Where the curve is flat, a small voltage error looks like a large SoC error, so the voltage is less useful there.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{fig2_ocv_curve.png}
  \caption[Resting voltage (OCV) of one cell against SoC.]{Resting voltage (OCV) of one cell against SoC. It is steep near empty and flat between about 20\% and 50\%. The dashed line is the straight-line approximation the EKF uses at 40\% SoC: good nearby, poor far away.}
  \label{fig:bg_ocv_tangent}
\end{figure}

\label{sec:bg_r0}\label{sec:bg_rc}\label{sec:bg_ecm}\textbf{Voltage under load.} When current $I$ flows (positive when discharging), the voltage drops below the resting voltage in two ways:
\begin{itemize}
  \item an \emph{instant drop} $R_0 I$ caused by the internal resistance $R_0$.\label{eq:bg_sag} At the 30\,A take-off current and $R_0 = 8$\,m$\Omega$ this is 0.24\,V per cell, so the battery looks emptier than it is;
  \item a \emph{slow drop} $V_1$ that builds up over about 30 seconds, modelled by a resistor $R_1$ in parallel with a capacitor $C_1$:
  \begin{equation}
    C_1 \frac{dV_1}{dt} = I - \frac{V_1}{R_1}, \qquad \tau_1 = R_1 C_1 = 30\text{\,s}.
    \label{eq:bg_rc}
  \end{equation}
\end{itemize}
Together these give the circuit model of Figure~\ref{fig:bg_circuit}. The voltage sensor reads the following, and only $V$ and $I$ are measured:
\begin{equation}
  V = \mathrm{OCV}(\mathrm{SoC}) - R_0\, I - V_1 .
  \label{eq:bg_terminal}
\end{equation}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.55\textwidth]{fig3_equivalent_circuit.png}
  \caption[Circuit model used by the EKF.]{Circuit model used by the EKF: the OCV, a resistor $R_0$ for the instant drop, and an $R_1$--$C_1$ pair for the slow drop. The simulated ``real'' battery has a second, slower $R$--$C$ pair (Section~\ref{sec:m_truth_vs_filter}).}
  \label{fig:bg_circuit}
\end{figure}

\label{sec:bg_cc}\textbf{Charge counting.} The simplest way to track the SoC is to start from a known value and subtract the charge used in each time step $k$ of length $\Delta t$ seconds (the 3600 converts ampere-seconds to ampere-hours):
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{SoC}_{k} = \mathrm{SoC}_{k-1} - \frac{I_k\, \Delta t}{3600\, C_n}.
  \label{eq:bg_cc}
\end{equation}
Charge counting never corrects itself. A wrong start stays wrong, a wrong capacity makes it drift, and a constant current-sensor error $b$ (the \emph{bias}) builds up over time:
\begin{equation}
  e(t) = -\frac{b\, t}{3600\, C_n}.
  \label{eq:bg_cc_drift}
\end{equation}
For example, a sensor reading 0.5\,A too high makes the SoC of a 5\,Ah battery 2.5\% too low after 15 minutes. Charge counting is used as a \emph{baseline}, a simple method the EKF should beat.

\section{Using the EKF to estimate the SoC}\label{sec:bg_kf}
%--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

\label{sec:bg_kf_idea}\label{sec:bg_ss}\label{sec:bg_qr}In this application, charge counting provides the prediction of the SoC and the measured voltage provides the correction. The general model is $x_k = f(x_{k-1}, u_k) + w_k$ and $z_k = h(x_k, u_k) + v_k$, where $w_k$ and $v_k$ are random errors with covariances $Q$ and $R_v$.\label{eq:bg_process} Table~\ref{tab:bg_mapping} lists what each part stands for here.

\begin{table}[htbp]
  \centering
  \caption{What each part of the EKF means in this project.}
  \label{tab:bg_mapping}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lp{0.68\textwidth}@{}}
    \toprule
    EKF term & In this project \\
    \midrule
    State $x_k$ & SoC and the slow voltage drop $V_1$ (Chapter~\ref{ch:method} adds three more battery values) \\
    Input $u_k$ & Measured current \\
    Measurement $z_k$ & Measured voltage \\
    Process model $f$ & Charge counting~\eqref{eq:bg_cc} and the build-up of $V_1$~\eqref{eq:bg_rc} \\
    Measurement model $h$ & Circuit equation~\eqref{eq:bg_terminal}: the voltage expected for a given SoC and current \\
    $Q$ & How much charge counting can be wrong in one step (mainly current-sensor noise) \\
    $R_v$ & How much the voltage reading can be wrong (sensor noise plus what the circuit model cannot explain) \\
    $P_k$ & Uncertainty of each state; the square root of its SoC entry is the ``$\pm$'' of the SoC estimate \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\label{sec:bg_kf_loop}\label{sec:bg_ekf}\label{eq:bg_kf_s}\label{eq:bg_kf_pupd}\label{eq:bg_jacobians}\textbf{One step of the filter.} Every second the filter does three things, using $F_k$ and $H_k$, the Jacobians (slopes) of $f$ and $h$ at the latest estimate, which replace the OCV curve with its tangent (Figure~\ref{fig:bg_ocv_tangent}).
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Predict the SoC with charge counting.} The current sensor is imperfect, so the uncertainty grows:
  \begin{equation}
    \hat{x}_k^- = f(\hat{x}_{k-1}, u_k), \qquad P_k^- = F_k P_{k-1} F_k^{\top} + Q.
    \label{eq:bg_kf_ppred}
  \end{equation}
  \item \textbf{Compare the expected and measured voltage.} The difference between the measured voltage and the voltage the circuit model expects is the \emph{innovation} $\tilde{y}_k$ (10\,mV less than expected suggests a little less charge), and $S_k$ is its expected size:
  \begin{equation}
    \tilde{y}_k = z_k - h(\hat{x}_k^-, u_k), \qquad S_k = H_k P_k^- H_k^{\top} + R_v.
    \label{eq:bg_kf_innov}
  \end{equation}
  \item \textbf{Correct the SoC.} The Kalman gain $K_k$ decides how much of the difference is used, and the uncertainty shrinks:
  \begin{equation}
    K_k = P_k^- H_k^{\top} S_k^{-1}, \qquad \hat{x}_k = \hat{x}_k^- + K_k \tilde{y}_k, \qquad P_k = (\mathbb{I} - K_k H_k) P_k^- .
    \label{eq:bg_kf_xupd}
  \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}

\label{sec:bg_gain}\textbf{What this means for the battery.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The voltage is only as useful as the OCV curve is steep.} The SoC entry of $H_k$ is the OCV slope, so the gain is large near empty. On the flat part, a 10\,mV error looks like a 2\% SoC error, so the gain is small and the uncertainty grows. The filter's ``$\pm$'' follows the curve, which the return-home rule uses later.
  \item \textbf{A low voltage can mean low charge or a large load.} At a steady current, less charge and a larger drop $R_0 I$ look the same. When the current changes, for example at the end of take-off, only $R_0 I$ changes, which lets the filter learn the resistance (Section~\ref{sec:m_ekf_investigation}).
\end{itemize}
