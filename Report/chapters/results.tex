\section{Simulator check}\label{sec:r_sim}
%--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The short mission, flown once without a filter, took 14.2\,min and used the battery from 95\% to 18.5\%. Figure~\ref{fig:r_voltage} shows the voltage sag that fools simple gauges: the measured voltage sits up to 0.37\,V below the resting voltage, jumps up when take-off ends and steps down at every hover, while the true SoC falls smoothly. Charge counting with the 0.5\,A sensor bias ended 2.3\% low, as \eqref{eq:bg_cc_drift} predicts (2.4\%). This run used the first, stronger slow RC pair ($R_2 = 4$\,m$\Omega$), whose widening gap led to the milder default (Section~\ref{sec:m_why_mismatch}).

In E2 (Figure~\ref{fig:r_soc_tracking}) the battery really started at 85\% but every estimator assumed full. Charge counting never noticed: at landing the battery held about 9\%, while charge counting believed about 21\%, which on a longer flight would mean running out while thinking a fifth of the battery was left. Stage~B corrected the wrong start within 42\,s. Its key moment, in both flights, was the end of take-off: during the steady 30\,A climb a low SoC and a high resistance look the same, and the sudden step to 15\,A told them apart. Stage~B learned $R_0 = 8.85$\,m$\Omega$ (true 8.8, starting from 8.0). Its innovations looked like random noise inside their expected size, with a mean NIS of 0.51, slightly cautious by design of the 10\,mV allowance.

\subsection{Stress tests (E3--E7)}\label{sec:r_day4_setup}

These runs used 10 averaged resting readings for the starting guess.

\label{sec:r_e3}\textbf{Sensor bias (E3).} With a 0.8\,A bias, charge counting ended 4.8\% low, while stages B and C stayed within 2.5\% (RMSE 1.28\% and 1.58\%). Stage~C learned the bias only partly (+0.33\,A against +0.8\,A, with a wide band): one flight is not enough to separate it from the resistance, but the SoC stays right because the voltage keeps correcting it.

\label{sec:r_e4}\textbf{Aged battery (E4).} This is the most important stress test (Figure~\ref{fig:r_e4}). With a battery at 85\% of its rated capacity, charge counting ended 10.2\% too high and stage~B 8.5\% too high, its band holding the truth only 28\% of the time: it blamed the lower voltage on its resistance instead of a smaller battery. Stage~C ended only 0.2\% off (RMSE 0.80\%), with its capacity estimate falling from 5.0 to 4.45\,Ah (true 4.25). Its bias estimate had the wrong sign, but together with the capacity it gave almost the right drain rate \eqref{eq:m_rate}, which is what the SoC depends on.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{day4_e4_aged.png}
  \caption{E4, aged battery. Top: SoC error; charge counting and stage B drift to about $+8$--$10\%$, stage C stays near zero. Bottom: stage C's capacity estimate moves towards the true 4.25\,Ah.}
  \label{fig:r_e4}
\end{figure}

\label{sec:r_e5}\textbf{Tuning (E5).} Telling the filter its voltage sensor was almost perfect ($R \times 0.01$) made it chase noise: it swung to $-13\%$ during take-off, was honest only 30\% of the time, and its NIS rose to 32, a clear alarm. Telling it the sensor was nearly useless ($R \times 100$) kept it honest but with bands far too wide, which would make a drone turn back much too early. $Q \times 100$ made it jumpy (RMSE 2.35\%). $Q \times 0.01$ was slightly more accurate (0.67\% against 1.06\%) but less honest (94\%), so the chosen tuning was kept.

\label{sec:r_e6}\textbf{Strong mismatch (E6).} With the stronger slow RC pair every filter degraded: stage~A ended 7.9\% low, stage~C drifted to about $-8\%$ before recovering to $-5\%$, and the best band (stage~C) held the truth 75\% of the time. Charge counting, which ignores the voltage, did about as well (RMSE 2.33\%). This is the honest limit of the method: an EKF is only as good as its model.

\label{sec:r_e7}\textbf{Flat OCV region (E7).} As the flight entered the flat part of the curve, where the slope falls from about 8 to 5\,mV per 1\% SoC, stage~C's uncertainty rose from 1.5\% to 2.2\%. Around 65\% SoC, where the slope has a small bump, the uncertainty dipped, following the slope closely. Stage~B's uncertainty kept shrinking instead, because learning the resistance kept adding information.

\subsection{Comparison over 20 flights (E8)}\label{sec:r_e8}

Table~\ref{tab:r_e8} averages 20 random flights per scenario. Stage~C is the only filter honest in all four normal scenarios (E1--E4), with RMSE between 1.0\% and 1.8\%. Stage~B is the most accurate on a healthy battery but fails on an aged one. Stage~A is never honest, even though its NIS looks healthy (about 0.5): its normalised SoC error (NEES) was 30 to 400 times the expected value of 1, so its own signals cannot reveal its bias. These results confirm stage~C as the filter for decisions.

\section{Return-to-home decisions on one flight}\label{sec:r_single_rth}
%--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The demonstration mission (aged battery, sensor reading 0.5\,A low, 10\% headwind) was flown with each policy (Table~\ref{tab:r_demo}, Figure~\ref{fig:r_demo_decision}). Charge counting sat about 10\% above the true SoC and kept drifting higher, so policy~B pushed on to a second delivery, turned back at 8.2\,min and died about 200\,m from home. Policies A and C turned back at 6.4\,min, right at the edge, and landed with just under 12\%. Policy~D turned back half a minute earlier, when its estimate minus its uncertainty met the charge needed to get home, and landed with 17.8\%.

\section{Monte Carlo study}\label{sec:r_mc}
%--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Table~\ref{tab:r_mc} gives the results of 500 random missions, flown by every policy under the same conditions and noise. Policy~D was the only one with no deaths and a reserve-breach rate (2.8\%) inside its 5\% promise. Policy~C, with the same filter but ignoring its uncertainty, aims exactly at the reserve and broke it in 38\% of missions. Policy~B was dangerous: it died in 30\% of all missions and 58\% of those with an aged battery. Policy~A died in 9\% of missions and still left 8\% of the battery unused. Over these missions the stage-C EKF tracked the SoC with an RMSE of 1.31\%, against 3.35\% for charge counting, and its band held the truth 100\% of the time.

\section{Safety versus wasted battery}\label{sec:r_sweep}
%--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Figure~\ref{fig:r_tradeoff} repeats 200 of the missions with different thresholds. Policy~D kept its promise at every setting: at 80\%, 90\%, 95\% and 99\% it broke the reserve in 9.5\%, 4.0\%, 2.0\% and 0\% of missions, each within the promised 20\%, 10\%, 5\% and 1\%, so its probabilities can be trusted. (With 200 missions, the 0.1\% promise at 99.9\% can be neither confirmed nor contradicted.) Going from 90\% to 99\% safety cost 3.2\% more unused battery. No voltage threshold was both safe and efficient: 3.40\,V killed 96\% of drones, while 3.55\,V, the setting that matches D's safety, wasted 17.9\% of the battery and completed half as many deliveries. For every reasonably safe voltage threshold, a setting of D was at least as safe and wasted less.
