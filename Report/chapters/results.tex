Two measures are used throughout: the \textbf{RMSE}, the typical size of the SoC error over a flight (lower is better), and \textbf{``in $\pm 3\sigma$''}, how often the true SoC was inside the filter's own band (close to 100\% for an honest filter). All SoC errors are in percentage points of SoC.

%--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Simulator check}\label{sec:r_sim}
%--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The short mission was first flown without the filter. It took 14.2\,min and used the battery from 95\% to 18.5\%. The measured voltage sat up to 0.37\,V below the resting voltage, jumped up when take-off ended and stepped down at every hover, while the true SoC fell smoothly, which is why a voltage reading alone is misleading. Charge counting with the 0.5\,A sensor bias ended 2.3\% too low, close to the 2.4\% predicted by~\eqref{eq:bg_cc_drift}. This run used the first, stronger slow $R$--$C$ pair, which led to the milder default (Section~\ref{sec:m_why_mismatch}).

\subsection{Normal flight and wrong start (E1, E2)}\label{sec:r_e1}\label{sec:r_e2}

Stage~B was the most accurate in both flights, with the true SoC always inside its band; stage~A read the SoC too low and was overconfident, as the early tests predicted. Here the start came from a single resting reading (91.9\% against a true 95\%), so in E1 charge counting began 3\% low before the bias drift added more.

\textbf{A wrong start.} In E2 the battery really started at 85\%, but every estimator assumed it was full (Figure~\ref{fig:r_soc_tracking}). Charge counting never noticed (at landing: about 9\% true, 21\% believed), while stage~B corrected the wrong start within 42\,s.

\textbf{The end of take-off} was the key moment for stage~B in both flights. During the steady 30\,A climb, a lower SoC and a higher resistance look the same (Section~\ref{sec:bg_kf}); when the current stepped down to 15\,A, they separated. The resistance estimate jumped at that moment from 8.0 to about 8.8\,m$\Omega$ and ended at 8.85\,m$\Omega$ (true 8.8).

\textbf{Health check.} The innovations looked like random noise of the expected size (Figure~\ref{fig:r_innovation}), with a mean NIS of 0.51, as expected.

\subsection{Stress tests (E3--E7)}\label{sec:r_day4_setup}

Each stress test makes one thing harder (start from 10 averaged resting readings).

\label{sec:r_e3}\textbf{Sensor bias (E3).} With the current sensor reading 0.8\,A too high, charge counting ended 4.8\% too low, while stages B and C stayed within 2.5\% (RMSE 1.28\% and 1.58\%). Stage~C estimated only part of the bias ($+0.33$\,A of $+0.8$\,A), since one flight cannot separate it from the resistance, but its SoC stayed right.

\label{sec:r_e4}\textbf{Aged battery (E4).} This is the most important stress test, because it is the dangerous case (Figure~\ref{fig:r_e4}): the battery held only 85\% of its rated capacity. Charge counting ended 10.2\% too high, and stage~B 8.5\% too high, with the true SoC inside its band only 28\% of the time. Stage~B cannot change its capacity, so it blamed the lower voltage on a higher resistance instead of a smaller battery. Stage~C ended only 0.2\% off (RMSE 0.80\%), and its capacity estimate fell from 5.0 to 4.45\,Ah (true value 4.25). Its bias estimate even had the wrong sign, but together with the capacity it gave almost the right drain rate~\eqref{eq:m_rate}, which is what the SoC depends on.

\label{sec:r_e5}\textbf{Tuning (E5).} With $R_v \times 0.01$ the filter believed its voltage sensor was almost perfect and chased the noise: it swung to 13\% too low during take-off, was honest only 30\% of the time, and its NIS rose to 32, a clear warning sign. With $R_v \times 100$ it stayed honest, but its band was far too wide for useful decisions. $Q \times 100$ made it jumpy (RMSE 2.35\%), and $Q \times 0.01$ was slightly more accurate (0.67\% vs 1.06\%) but less honest (94\%), so the chosen tuning was kept.

\label{sec:r_e6}\textbf{Strong model difference (E6).} With the stronger slow pair every filter got worse: over 20 flights (Table~\ref{tab:r_e8}) even stage~C covered the truth only 83\% of the time, and charge counting beat every filter (RMSE 1.83\% against 2.37--5.10\%). An EKF is only as good as its model.

\label{sec:r_e7}\textbf{Flat part of the curve (E7).} As the flight entered the flat part of the OCV curve, where the slope falls from about 8 to 5\,mV per 1\% of SoC, stage~C's uncertainty rose from 1.5\% to 2.2\% (Figure~\ref{fig:r_e7}). It dipped near 65\% SoC, where the curve has a small bump, so the filter becomes less sure exactly where the voltage tells it less. Stage~B's uncertainty kept shrinking, as estimating the resistance kept adding information.

\subsection{Comparison over 20 flights (E8)}\label{sec:r_e8}

E8 repeats five scenarios over 20 random flights each (Table~\ref{tab:r_e8}).
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Stage C} is the only filter honest in all four normal scenarios (E1--E4), with RMSE 1.0--1.8\%.
  \item \textbf{Stage B} is the most accurate on a healthy battery but fails on an aged one (E4).
  \item \textbf{Stage A} is never honest, even though its NIS looked normal (about 0.5): its NEES was 30 to 400, so its error was about 5 to 20 times its own $\sigma$, and a real drone could not detect this from its own signals. Its good E4 accuracy is a coincidence: its low reading from the resistance error partly cancels the smaller battery's high one.
\end{itemize}
These results confirm stage~C as the filter for the return-home decision.

\section{Return-home decisions on one flight}\label{sec:r_single_rth}
%--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The demonstration mission (aged battery, sensor 0.5\,A low, 10\% headwind) was flown with each policy (Figure~\ref{fig:r_demo_decision}).
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Counting} thought the battery was about 10\% fuller than it was, and the gap kept growing. It pushed on to a second delivery, turned back at 8.2\,min, and the battery died about 200\,m from home.
  \item \textbf{Voltage} and \textbf{EKF-mean} turned back at 6.4\,min, right at the limit, and landed with 11.7\% and 11.9\%.
  \item \textbf{EKF-risk} turned back half a minute earlier, when the estimate minus its uncertainty met the charge needed to get home, and landed with 17.8\%.
\end{itemize}

\section{Monte Carlo study}\label{sec:r_mc}
%--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

All four policies flew the same 500 random missions (Table~\ref{tab:r_mc}).
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{EKF-risk}: no battery failures, and a reserve-breach rate (2.8\%) within its 5\% promise.
  \item \textbf{EKF-mean} (same filter, uncertainty ignored): aims at the reserve and fell below it in 38\%.
  \item \textbf{Counting}: the battery died in 30\% of missions (58\% with an aged battery).
  \item \textbf{Voltage}: the battery died in 9\% of missions, yet 8\% was left unused on average.
\end{itemize}
The stage~C EKF's SoC RMSE was 1.31\% (charge counting: 3.35\%), with the truth always inside its band.

\label{sec:r_sweep}\textbf{Safety versus wasted battery.} Repeating 200 missions with other settings (Figure~\ref{fig:r_tradeoff}) showed that EKF-risk never broke its promise: at 80, 90, 95 and 99\% it broke the reserve in 9.5, 4.0, 2.0 and 0\% of missions, against the promised 20, 10, 5 and 1\%. It is about twice as safe as promised (cautious tuning); going from 90\% to 99\% cost 3.2\% more unused battery. No voltage threshold was safe and efficient: 3.40\,V let 96\% of batteries die, and 3.55\,V, as safe as EKF-risk, wasted 17.9\% and halved the deliveries.
