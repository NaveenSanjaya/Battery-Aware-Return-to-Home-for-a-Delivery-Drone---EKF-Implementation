\section{Why the filter works, and where it is unsure}\label{sec:d_why}

Charge counting has no way to notice its own mistakes, so a wrong start, a sensor bias or a smaller battery stay wrong for the whole flight. The EKF gets an independent check from the voltage every second, and the Kalman gain decides how much to trust it (Section~\ref{sec:bg_gain}). That check is weakest where the OCV curve is flat: there a voltage error looks like a large SoC error, so the gain is small and the uncertainty grows. Experiment E7 showed exactly this, with stage~C's uncertainty rising as the slope fell and dipping where the slope briefly rose. The filter knows when it knows less, which is what the return-to-home rule needs.

\section{What one flight can and cannot reveal}\label{sec:d_observability}

The resistance shows itself whenever the current changes, because only the sag changes with the current while the OCV does not; in E1 and E2 the end of take-off was enough to learn it. The bias and the capacity are different. Within one flight of mostly steady cruise their effects on the voltage overlap with each other and with the resistance \eqref{eq:m_confound}, so they cannot be told apart, and stage~C's individual estimates were sometimes wrong, even in sign (E3, E4). What the voltage does fix is their combination, the drain rate \eqref{eq:m_rate}, and that is all the SoC and the trip cost need. This is why the trip cost uses the rate and not the capacity alone.

\section{Model errors and tuning}\label{sec:d_mismatch}

The largest errors came from the model, not from the sensors. A resistance that is 10\% wrong makes the filter consistently misread the voltage; this is not random noise, so telling the filter its sensor is noisier does not remove it (Section~\ref{sec:m_ekf_investigation}). Worse, the filter cannot see the problem itself: stage~A's innovations looked healthy (NIS about 0.5) while its error, checked against the truth, was 30 to 400 times larger than it claimed (E8). Only a simulation, where the truth is known, exposes this. Learning the resistance removed most of the error; the strong mismatch of E6 shows that a large enough model error defeats every version of the filter.

Tuning sets how cautious the filter is rather than how accurate. A too-small $R$ made it chase noise, and its NIS of 32 raised the alarm; a too-large $R$ kept it honest but would make the drone turn back far too early (E5). The chosen settings are slightly cautious on purpose, because the decision rule turns the filter's uncertainty directly into a safety margin.

A more advanced filter would not fix the main problem. With a perfect model the EKF's error was only 0.12\%, so the tangent-line approximation is not what limits accuracy; the model is. An unscented Kalman filter or a particle filter~\cite{simon2006optimal,purdue_jate_comparison} would cost more computation and still inherit the same model error.

\section{The most accurate filter is not the safest}\label{sec:d_choice}\label{sec:d_value}

Stage~B was the most accurate filter on a healthy battery, yet on an aged battery it was confidently wrong, and wrong in the dangerous direction: it believed there was more charge than there was (E4, E8). Stage~C was slightly less accurate on a healthy battery but honest in every normal scenario. For a decision that depends on the uncertainty, honesty matters more than the last fraction of a percent of accuracy, so stage~C drives the return-to-home rule.

The Monte Carlo study shows the value of using that uncertainty. Policies C and D use the same filter and differ only in whether they use its uncertainty. Policy~C aims exactly at the reserve, so it lands below it about as often as above, breaking it in 38\% of missions. Policy~D keeps a buffer that grows when the filter is unsure, and broke the reserve in 2.8\% of missions, inside its 5\% promise (Table~\ref{tab:r_mc}). At every threshold the share of missions that broke the reserve stayed within the promise (Figure~\ref{fig:r_tradeoff}), so the probabilities mean what they say. This tests the whole chain at once: the model, the filter's uncertainty, the trip cost and the probability. The price is modest: about 6\% of the battery unused and slightly fewer deliveries than policy~C, and an operator can choose a different threshold to move along the trade-off.

The baselines fail for clear reasons. Charge counting becomes optimistic with an aged battery or a sensor that reads low, and died in 58\% of aged-battery missions. A voltage threshold ignores how far the drone is from home and is fooled by sag, so no single threshold is both safe and efficient.

\section{Limitations and future work}\label{sec:d_limitations}\label{sec:d_future}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Simulation only.} The results come from a simulated battery and drone; real flight logs, such as the public datasets~\cite{cmu_dji_dataset,cmu_evtol_paper}, do not contain the true SoC, but could test the voltage predictions.
  \item \textbf{Representative battery data.} The OCV curve is a typical lithium-ion shape, not measured on a specific cell; temperature and voltage hysteresis are not modelled.
  \item \textbf{Simple decision model.} The rule assumes bell-shaped errors, a straight flight home and a constant cruise current, and does not add the uncertainty of the drain rate itself.
  \item \textbf{One flight is not enough} to learn the bias and capacity separately. Calibrating the current sensor at rest before take-off, when the true current is zero, and estimating the capacity between flights from the charge used and the resting voltage before and after, would fix both cheaply.
  \item \textbf{Model error.} A two-RC filter model, or noise settings that adapt during flight, could reduce the errors seen under strong mismatch (E6).
\end{itemize}
