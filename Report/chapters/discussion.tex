Notes from Day 3 (to expand):
\begin{itemize}
  \item E1 and E2 show the observability argument in a real run (Section~\ref{sec:r_e1}): during the constant-current take-off, SoC and $R_0$ cannot be separated, and the stage-B band stays wide (about $\pm 8.5\%$). The current step from 30\,A to 15\,A at about 0.5\,min is the excitation that separates them: $\partial h/\partial R_0 = -(I_m - b)$ changes with the current, while $d\,\mathrm{OCV}/d\,\mathrm{SoC}$ does not. The error jumps, the band collapses to about $\pm 2.5\%$ and $R_0$ moves to the true value at that moment.
  \item Consequence for operations: a stage-B filter needs a change in current before it is trustworthy. Take-off and hover provide this naturally; a flight with constant current would not. Its wide band during take-off is honest, and the RTH rule would treat it cautiously.
  \item In E2 the same effect slows stage B's recovery from a wrong start (30\,s at about $+5$--$7\%$), while stage A, which has no $R_0$ to confuse with SoC, corrects the start at once but then drifts. This trade-off between immediate correction and long-term accuracy is worth explaining.
\end{itemize}

Notes from Day 3 (to expand):
\begin{itemize}
  \item Stage B still ends E1 at $-1.53\%$, drifting slowly low over the flight. This is attributed to the unmodelled slow RC pair ($R_2 = 1.5$\,m$\Omega$, $\tau_2 = 400$\,s), which builds up over the flight and is partly read as lower SoC. The error stays inside the $\pm 3\sigma$ band, so the uncertainty is honest. E6 (with $R_2 = 4$\,m$\Omega$) will show how this grows with a larger mismatch.
  \item Stage A had a mean NIS of 0.54 (E1) although its SoC was biased and its band contained the truth only 17\% of the time. Innovation-based checks only test whether the voltage is explained, and a wrong SoC can explain it. Only truth-based checks (NEES, $\pm 3\sigma$ coverage), available in simulation, reveal this. This is a limitation of checking a real filter from flight logs alone.
  \item The E1 resting-voltage guess was about 3\% low from a single noisy reading. Averaging several readings before take-off would reduce this; worth mentioning as a cheap improvement.
\end{itemize}

Notes from Day 4 (MATLAB results, to expand):
\begin{itemize}
  \item E6 confirms the limit: with the strong slow RC pair ($R_2 = 4$\,m$\Omega$), no stage is consistent (coverage 2--75\% in the single run, at most 83\% over 20 flights), and stage~B only matches charge counting (2.37\% vs 1.83\% mean RMSE). Stage~C drifted to about $-8\%$ and left its band. When the model is too wrong, the voltage feedback misleads the filter. Remedies: a two-RC filter model, a larger $\sigma_{\text{model}}$, or adaptive noise estimation (e.g.\ raising $R$ when the innovations show a trend).
  \item E5: NIS detects a mistuned $R$ ($R{\times}0.01$: mean NIS 32.3, coverage 30\%), but it cannot detect model bias. Over 20 flights stage~A had a mean NIS of about 0.5 in E1 but a mean NEES of 91.5. NIS checks the voltage, not the hidden state, so a real drone, without the true SoC, could not detect this kind of failure from its own innovations.
  \item E5: $Q{\times}0.01$ was more accurate in its single run (0.67\% vs 1.06\%) but less honest (94\%). The chosen tuning trades a little accuracy for margin, because the RTH rule relies on $\sigma_{\text{SoC}}$.
  \item E7: stage~C's $\sigma_{\text{SoC}}$ grows from about 1.5\% to about 2.2\% as the OCV slope falls, and dips exactly where the slope has a local bump near 65--70\% SoC, which is direct evidence that the voltage information sets the uncertainty. Stage~B's uncertainty falls steadily (about 0.95\% to 0.70\%), because information keeps accumulating through $R_0$. The stated uncertainty depends on the whole filter structure.
\end{itemize}

\section{Choosing the filter for decisions}\label{sec:d_choice}
\todo{Expand into the main discussion section of the report, using the notes below.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Stage~B is the most accurate on a healthy battery (E8 mean RMSE 1.03\% in E1), but on the aged battery (E4) its $R_0$ state absorbs the voltage shortfall caused by the capacity error. Its SoC error grew almost parallel to charge counting's, to $+8.34\%$ on average at landing, with 27\% coverage and a mean NEES of 45.6. It was confidently optimistic, the dangerous direction for an RTH decision.
  \item Stage~C was the only filter with 100\% coverage in all of E1--E4, with mean RMSE 1.03--1.80\%. It pays a small accuracy price on a healthy battery (1.41\% vs 1.03\% in E1) for robustness.
  \item Why stage~C works although its parameters are wrong: only the discharge rate $(I_m - b)/C_n$ is observable within one flight. In E4 it ended with $C_n = 4.45$\,Ah (true 4.25) and $b = -0.42$\,A (true $+0.50$), yet its rate was within about 1.5\% of the truth and its final SoC error was $+0.17\%$. In E3 its bias estimate ($+0.33$\,A vs $+0.80$\,A) stayed honestly uncertain ($\pm 1.8$\,A band).
  \item Main lesson: the most accurate filter is not always the safest one to make decisions with. A decision filter should be judged by the honesty of its uncertainty across the conditions it will meet, not only by its accuracy in the nominal case.
  \item Future work: calibrate the current-sensor bias at rest before take-off (known zero current), and estimate capacity between flights from ampere-hour throughput and the SoC read from the resting voltage before and after, which would make $b$ and $C_n$ individually observable.
\end{itemize}

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:d_limitations}
\todo{Discuss each limitation openly, and link it to the results where possible:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Temperature is not modelled; a cold battery has higher resistance and less usable capacity.
  \item Voltage hysteresis between charge and discharge is ignored.
  \item Capacity and sensor bias are only weakly observable within one flight; only their combined effect on the discharge rate is learned (E3, E4).
  \item Under a strong model mismatch (E6) no filter is consistent, and the voltage feedback can make the EKF worse than charge counting.
  \item The probability rule assumes Gaussian errors; heavy-tailed events (a gust, a motor fault) are not covered.
  \item The rule assumes a straight flight home at a constant cruise current, an exact GPS position, and treats the capacity estimate as exact (Section~\ref{sec:m_rth_prob}).
  \item The remaining systematic model errors are covered only by a fixed allowance $\sigma_{\text{model}}$ in the measurement noise (Section~\ref{sec:m_ekf_h}), which treats them as if they were random.
  \item The current-sensor noise that enters the measurement equation is correlated with the process noise; this correlation is ignored.
  \item All results come from simulation; real flight logs have no true SoC to compare against.
\end{itemize}
